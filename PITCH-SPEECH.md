# Schoolynq pitch: speaker script

For the 13-slide Schoolynq pitch in the Presenter. About 14 minutes of speaking, plus the live demos.
Lines in [square brackets] are actions; the buttons are on the slides. With “Present with laptop screen”, a short version of each slide’s points shows on your laptop.

## 1 · Ready lessons for teachers. Clear progress for school leaders.

Thank you for making time for us. My colleagues and I come from teaching and curriculum backgrounds, and Schoolynq grew out of something we kept running into in our own classrooms.

In one line: Schoolynq gives teachers ready lessons, and gives school leaders a clear view of how learning is going. **Missions** covers Grades 1 to 8, on the classroom screen. **Polaris** covers Grades 9 to 12, on each student’s own device. Both are built on the Cambridge curriculum and on the books and calendar your school already uses.

I’ll start with the problem, show you how we prepare lessons and what teachers and leaders get, and finish with a small, practical way to try it.

## 2 · There’s a lot behind a great 40-minute lesson

There’s a lot of work behind a really good forty-minute lesson.

Knowing the subject is only part of it. Someone still has to decide how to introduce the idea, find a visual that makes it clear, prepare practice with a purpose, and ask a question that shows whether students actually understood.

Experienced teachers do this well. But doing it consistently, for every lesson and every section, takes a great deal of preparation, on top of marking and everything else in a school week.

So our question was simple: how can we make strong lesson preparation easier to deliver, day after day?

## 3 · More from your teachers, resources and classroom technology

Your school has already invested in good teachers, classroom screens and learning resources. We want to help your teachers get more from that investment.

A classroom screen can do more than show slides or play a video. Students can change something and see what happens. In Science, they might follow blood through the heart. In Maths, they might stretch a shape and watch its area change.

What matters is what students do next. Can they explain it? Can they answer a question about it? Can they apply it again next week?

So the visuals sit alongside reading, discussion and written practice. They give the teacher one more way to explain a difficult idea.

## 4 · AI may know the topic. Does it know the lesson?

Teachers now also have AI. That opens up a lot, but access alone doesn’t produce a consistent lesson.

AI may know the topic. *Does it know the lesson?*

Does it know the objective for this grade? What the class covered last week? How deeply your school expects the topic to be taught? Which misconception to tackle? What students should be able to do by the end of the period?

The result depends on those details, and on someone checking the material and fitting it into teaching.

That is the work we do: explanations, visuals, activities, practice and checks on understanding, organised into a teaching sequence for your classes.

So what do we need from your school?

## 5 · Prepared from the materials your school already uses

Four things you already have.

- Your **syllabus** tells us what students need to learn, and how deep to go.
- Your **books** give us the content, vocabulary, examples and practice students work with.
- Your **academic calendar** shows how teaching fits around term dates, events, assessments and revision.
- Your **weekly routine** tells us how often each subject is taught, and how much time there is.

From those, we build the teaching plan and prepare the lesson resources. You don’t need to write a finished teaching plan for us first.

Your teachers teach with those resources, and your academic team can see progress and where follow-up is needed.

Let me show you what that looks like in a lesson.

## 6 · A lesson ready to teach

[Click “Open the Heart mission”.]

This is a Mission a teacher would use for an upcoming Science lesson. Every Mission has the same shape: explain, explore, check, practise.

The teacher opens it with the notes and worksheet already in place. Inside, the lesson moves in a prepared sequence: a visual explanation, something for students to do, and questions that tell the teacher whether to move on or go back.

Here, I’ll pause when the blood reaches the lungs and ask:

> “The blood has picked up oxygen. Why does it need to go back to the heart?”

The model gives students something concrete to reason about, and their answers tell the teacher far more than “Does everyone understand?” If they can explain the next step, we move on. If they can’t, we know exactly which part to explain again.

## 7 · The class sees the lesson. You see the plan.

[Optional: open the Blood mission and choose “Start with teacher screen”.]

With two screens, the class sees only the lesson. The teacher’s laptop shows the plan: talking points and timings for each step, the misconceptions to expect, and a run sheet so nothing is missed.

When the teacher asks a question, the spotlight picks students in turn, so it isn’t always the same hands, and each answer is recorded against the student’s name.

In fact, this presentation is running the same way. You’re seeing the slides; I’m seeing my notes.

## 8 · Notes and worksheets, ready to print

Not all learning happens on a screen. Each Mission comes with pupil notes, a worksheet with square-grid and ruled space for working, exit slips with an answer key, and a teacher guide.

Teachers print what they need, so the written practice matches what was taught on the board.

## 9 · Shared objectives. Flexible teaching.

[Click “Open the lesson planner”.]

Across parallel sections, teachers work towards the same core objectives and use the same check on understanding. Each teacher still has room to explain differently, spend longer on a hard point and respond to their class.

The school can also set its preferred lesson rhythm. For a forty-minute period, that might be twenty minutes of teaching, fifteen of practice and five of review. That’s only an example; we build the material around your expectations.

The planner ties the sequence to your calendar and weekly routine. When a lesson runs long, or a school event takes a day, the plan is re-paced against the time left and the next assessment.

That gives teachers and coordinators a shared starting point for talking about coverage, pacing and classroom time.

## 10 · A clearer view across your classrooms

[Click “Open the principal dashboard”.]

For coordinators and school leaders, this dashboard brings together coverage, pacing, teacher usage and student responses across sections. The school and the numbers in this demo are invented, but the questions are real.

Are sections progressing as planned? Where is evidence of understanding missing? Is a difficulty showing up in one class, or across the grade?

These signals need reading together. A section that finished the material hasn’t necessarily understood it. A section that took longer wasn’t necessarily taught badly. That’s why the evidence sits alongside professional judgement, not in place of it.

Used well, it tells a coordinator where to look first: to identify a gap, share an explanation that worked, or give a teacher specific support and then follow up.

## 11 · Know where students need help

[Click “Open this class”, then “Open Sarah’s profile”.]

For the class teacher, the question is more immediate: *what do I need to teach again tomorrow, and who needs help?*

Here we look across the class lesson by lesson, which shows whether a concept is troubling most of the class or a small group. Then we open one student.

Sarah missed the Body systems lesson because she was absent, so there’s no class evidence for her yet. Her next step is a catch-up pack before the block assessment. A student who was there, did the work and still keeps misunderstanding the same idea needs something different: another explanation and targeted practice.

Responses sit alongside homework, tests, attendance and teacher notes. The aim is always a specific next step, and then a check that it helped.

We’d fit this to your existing academic processes. Your coordinators and teachers decide which information is useful and how it’s used.

## 12 · Polaris: understand the concept, apply it independently

Polaris is for a different stage. In the senior years, following an explanation in class is only the beginning. Students also need to go back over the syllabus on their own, and apply what they know to unfamiliar questions.

For example, a student follows a lesson on velocity–time graphs today. A week later, can they tell when a question needs the gradient, and when it needs the area?

Polaris brings together syllabus-based learning, independent revision and exam practice, with simulations where they help. When a student gets something wrong, they get follow-up on exactly that idea.

[Click “Simulation Lab”, or open a senior Physics topic. Show the explanation or simulation, then the question.]

It gives students a clear route back into a topic, and a way to check they can really use it.

## 13 · Start with an 8-week pilot

[Leave this slide up for the discussion.]

My suggestion is to start small, with a clearly defined pilot: **eight weeks, two classes, in Maths and Science, with one coordinator.**

Before week one, we agree the classes, the chapters coming up, what our team provides, what teachers need to do, and what would make the pilot worthwhile for you. You share the syllabus pages and calendar; we prepare the lessons and train the teachers.

- In **weeks one and two**, our team is in the room for the first lessons, and we take a baseline.
- In **weeks three to six**, the Missions run in normal lessons, with a review together at week four.
- In **weeks seven and eight**, we look at the results against that baseline. Did it save preparation time? Did it work in the classroom? Did it show where students were struggling? Did it give the academic team something useful to act on? We write up what worked and what needs changing.

Then you decide: expand, adjust or stop. Even if you stop, your team keeps useful findings about those lessons and those students.

If your senior Physics team is interested, we’d also be glad to show them Polaris separately.

So, a small first step: would it make sense for us to show your coordinator and two teachers a lesson for a chapter they’re about to teach?
