Why return to the heart? · Teacher guide

Goal: pupils trace body → right side of heart → lungs → left side of heart → body, and explain that each heart visit supplies the push for the next part of the journey.

Prepare: one shared screen (mouse, touch or a presentation clicker), paper and pencils, one exit slip per pupil (Files → Paper practice and exit slip). Type the class names on the launch page for the student spotlight. No pupil devices, no sign-in, no internet.

Timing: 20 min guided explanation and interaction (00–20) · 15 min practice and application (20–35) · 5 min retrieval, correction and exit (35–40). Nothing advances automatically.

The three-minute pitch demonstration

  1. Open Schoolynq_Stage6_Heart_Player_v4.html. Press F11 for full screen. Select Jump to the key moment.
  2. Select ▶ Play journey. One red blood cell travels from the body to the right side of the heart. The heart squeezes and pushes it to the lungs. In the lungs, orange oxygen particles move into the cell, grey carbon dioxide leaves, and the cell turns from blue to red. It stops.
  3. The screen asks: “The blood has picked up oxygen. Why does it need to go back to the heart?” Hold the pause: silent thinking, partner talk, two predictions. Nothing moves until you choose.
  4. Select Reveal why: “Oxygen is cargo, not a pump. After the lungs the blood has lost most of its push.” Then ▶ Return to heart: the cell enters the left side.
  5. Select ▶ Pump to body. The left side glows and squeezes, “PUSH!” appears, and the cell is driven out along the artery. In the body capillaries oxygen and food move into body cells and the cell turns blue again.
  6. Select Check understanding →: the student spotlight names who answers Q4, and their score appears at the top.
  7. Say: “The lungs supply oxygen. The heart supplies the push.” Then open Lesson map → One heart, two sides to show the full simulation (both sides beating together), and Build the whole route (choose Lungs first to show corrective feedback).
“There’s a lot of work behind a really good forty-minute class.” Point to what sits behind this one moment: an explanation, a purposeful simulation, a paused prediction, practice with feedback, a paper exit check and teacher prompts for every screen.

Teacher prompts for every screen

01 · Blood is the delivery service (00–03 min)

Ask: Two minutes only. Pupils name what blood carries. Select Red cells, then Plasma. Ask: “So what keeps this delivery moving?”

Listen for: Oxygen and food delivered; waste (carbon dioxide) taken away. Red cells carry most of the oxygen; plasma is the liquid that carries food and waste.

Expected reasoning: Blood transports materials, but it cannot move itself. That sets up the need for a pump.

Likely misconception: Blood makes oxygen; or blood is only red cells.

If pupils struggle: Select each component once. White cells and platelets: one sentence each. Do not reteach the Blood lesson.

02 · A delivery needs a pump (04–07 min)

Ask: Start with the model still. Ask what could move the blood. Start the heartbeat, then pause the pump and restart it. Use “One squeeze” to show a single beat.

Listen for: The heart muscle contracts (squeezes) and pushes blood. Without the squeeze, the blood stops.

Expected reasoning: The heart is a muscular pump. Contraction supplies the force that moves blood through vessels.

Likely misconception: Blood moves by itself, or oxygen pushes the blood.

If pupils struggle: Pupils clench and relax a fist to feel a squeeze. Press “One squeeze” and ask which part supplied the push.

03 · One heart, two sides, four chambers (08–12 min)

Ask: Step through the five reveals. Let the simulation run; use Slow motion when you want pupils to track colour changes. Select a chamber, a valve or the wall for a one-line explanation. Chamber names are supporting vocabulary: atrium = upper, collects; ventricle = lower, pumps.

Listen for: Right side → lungs; left side → body; atrium collects, ventricle pumps; both sides squeeze together; the wall stops mixing; two heart visits per trip.

Expected reasoning: Two pumps in one organ, each sending blood to a different destination, beating together. The ventricles do the big push.

Likely misconception: Viewer’s right = person’s right; the sides take turns; blood mixes inside the heart; the atria do the main pumping.

If pupils struggle: Hold up your own right hand facing the class. Go back to step 1 and point to each chamber in the order blood visits it.

04 · Why return to the heart? (13–19 min)

Ask: Play journey. It stops at the lungs with the question on screen. Give 20 seconds silent thinking, 40 seconds partner talk, then take two predictions. Only then select Reveal why → Return to heart → Pump to body.

Listen for: “It needs the heart to pump it to the body.” “The lungs can’t push it.” Any explanation that names the heart’s pumping job.

Expected reasoning: The lungs supply oxygen; the heart supplies the push. The left side pumps the oxygen-rich blood onwards.

Likely misconception: “It already has oxygen, so it can go straight to the body.”

If pupils struggle: Reset and replay from the lungs. Ask two separate questions: where does oxygen enter? What gives the next push?

05 · Build the whole route (20–24 min)

Ask: Pairs agree each step and must give a reason before you select it. Choose one wrong answer on purpose (e.g. Lungs first) to show the feedback.

Listen for: Body → right side → lungs → left side → body, with a reason at each step.

Expected reasoning: Each heart side has a different destination; the route repeats.

Likely misconception: Skipping the second heart visit (lungs → body).

If pupils struggle: Use the sentence frame: “The right side pumps to … ; the left side pumps to …”. Reset and rebuild.

06 · Vessels and the oxygen swap (25–29 min)

Ask: Step 1: trace two vessels with your finger. Steps 2–3: run each close-up for 30 seconds; use Slow motion. Step 4 is an extension challenge.

Listen for: Artery = away, vein = towards; oxygen moves into blood in the lungs and out to cells at the body.

Expected reasoning: Thin capillary walls let oxygen, food and waste move between blood and air sacs or cells.

Likely misconception: All veins carry oxygen-poor blood; blood is really blue.

If pupils struggle: Re-run the lungs close-up and say the swap aloud together.

07 · Repair the missing pump (30–34 min)

Ask: Pupils sketch their repair on paper first. Then select Show the repaired route.

Listen for: Add the left side of the heart, with the reason “it pumps the blood to the body”.

Expected reasoning: A correct route explains both where oxygen is collected and what pushes the blood onwards.

Likely misconception: The lungs push blood to the body.

If pupils struggle: Partners point to the oxygen source, then to the pump.

08 · Your route. Your reason. (35–38 min)

Ask: Silent, individual, on paper. Collect or check every slip BEFORE you select Reveal the route.

Listen for: Lungs → left side of heart → body/foot, plus a reason that explicitly mentions pumping.

Expected reasoning: The left side of the heart pumps oxygen-rich blood onwards.

Likely misconception: Correct place names with no explanation of pumping.

If pupils struggle: Revisit the key moment next lesson for pupils missing either the route or the pumping reason.

Answers, exit check and scope

CheckQuestion · answerReason · likely misconception
Q1 · BWhich part of blood carries most of the oxygen?
Red blood cells		Red blood cells carry most of the oxygen. Plasma carries dissolved food and waste.
Common wrong choice: Plasma carries the oxygen.
Q2 · CWhat gives blood the push to leave the heart?
The heart muscle contracts (squeezes).		The heart is a muscular pump. When its muscle contracts, it pushes blood into the blood vessels.
Common wrong choice: Oxygen or the blood itself provides the push.
Q3 · AWhich sentence about the two sides of the heart is correct?
The right side pumps blood to the lungs. The left side pumps blood to the body.		The right side pumps blood to the lungs; the left side pumps blood to the body. Both sides squeeze together and the wall keeps their blood apart.
Common wrong choice: Option D (taking turns) is a common result of watching a single-cell animation.
Q4 · CWhy does oxygen-rich blood go back from the lungs to the heart?
So the left side of the heart can pump it around the body.		The lungs supply oxygen. The heart supplies the push. The left side of the heart pumps the oxygen-rich blood around the body.
Common wrong choice: A or B: oxygen or the lungs drive the blood.
Q5 · BWhich route takes blood from the body and back again?
Body → right side of heart → lungs → left side of heart → body		Body → right side → lungs → left side → body. Each side of the heart pumps blood to the next place.
Common wrong choice: Option A: skipping the right side.
Q6 · AWhat happens as blood passes through the capillaries in the lungs?
Oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out.		In the lung capillaries oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out.
Common wrong choice: Blood really turns blue; the lungs pump.
Q7 · DA route sends oxygen-rich blood straight from the lungs to a foot. Which correction explains the missing step?
Add the left side of the heart, because it pumps blood to the foot.		Lungs → left side of heart → body. Collecting oxygen does not replace the need for the heart’s push.
Common wrong choice: Vessels or lungs do the pumping.
Q8 · AA red blood cell is in a toe. Which journey comes next?
vein → right side of heart → lungs → left side of heart → artery		Toe → vein → right side → lungs → left side → artery → body. Two heart visits, each giving a push.
Common wrong choice: Starting with an artery; going to the lungs before the heart.

Paper exit slip: look for two things

Route: lungs → left side of heart → body/foot, with arrows. Reason: the heart pumps the blood onwards. A correct route without a pumping reason is incomplete.

Collect or check every slip before you reveal the route. Spotlight scores count only the questions each named student answered aloud; the paper exit slip is the check for every pupil.

Student spotlight and scores

Type the class names on the launch page (six demo names are filled in). Before each question the player calls one student by name, picked fairly: nobody twice in a row and fewest answers first. Use “Pick another student” if someone is absent. The teacher selects that student’s answer; the score chips at the top update, the lesson ends on a scoreboard, and “Save class progress” (also in Files) downloads the roster, scores and every answer as a JSON file.

Support and extension

Support: picture labels (body, right side, lungs, left side) and the frame “It goes back to the heart because the heart …”. Extension: explain why the vessel from the lungs to the heart is a vein even though its blood is oxygen-rich; explain what the valves do (one-way flow).

Four chambers

The model shows four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle. Atria (upper) collect blood; ventricles (lower) pump it out; valves keep the flow one way; the left ventricle has the thickest muscle. These names are supporting vocabulary, not assessed in the checks (Workbook Unit 1.1 only requires the two sides).

Scientific accuracy notes

Front view: the person’s right side is on the viewer’s left. Both sides of the heart contract at the same time; the slow model follows one cell. Blue is a diagram code for oxygen-poor blood: real blood is always red, and oxygen-poor blood still contains some oxygen (the Workbook’s phrase “without oxygen” is a simplification). In each beat the atria squeeze slightly before the ventricles, as in a real heart. The heartbeat in the model is slowed down and is not a real heart rate. The diagram is simplified: blood also goes to the head and arms, and vessel positions are schematic, but every connection and direction is correct.
